    How we built this

    What our data says

    EDA (Exploratory Data Analysis) means looking at the data with charts and simple numbers before building the model, like studying the ingredients before cooking. We used 999 people from two survey datasets (277 with PCOS, 722 without). Here is what we found, chart by chart.

    1. How many people have PCOS?

    What this means: Only 28 percent of our people have PCOS. A lazy guess of “no PCOS” for everyone would be right 72 percent of the time but would find nobody. So accuracy alone is not enough, and we also check how many real PCOS cases the model catches (recall).

    2. Which signs go with PCOS?

    What this means: Irregular periods stand out: 64 percent of people with irregular periods have PCOS, against 16 percent with regular periods. Extra hair growth (57 vs 17 percent), dark skin patches (51 vs 17) and weight gain (44 vs 16) also raise the chance a lot. Hair loss matters less, and exercise hardly changes anything.

    3. More signs, higher chance

    What this means: With none of five signs (weight gain, hair growth, skin darkening, hair loss, pimples) only about 7 percent have PCOS. With three it is about 45 percent, and with all five about 73 percent. No single sign proves PCOS, but the signs add up, which is why the model weighs them together.

    4. Body measurements: PCOS vs no PCOS

    What this means: Each box shows the middle half of the people. Weight and BMI sit a little higher in the PCOS group (median BMI 25.1 vs 23.1). Age (median 28) and period length (median 5 days) look almost the same in both groups, so they are weak clues on their own.

    5. How BMI and age are spread out

    What this means: The PCOS curve for BMI is shifted slightly to the right (average 25.3 vs 23.4) but the two curves overlap a lot, so BMI alone cannot tell the groups apart. Most people in our data are young: the median age is 28.

    6. What is most linked to PCOS?

    What this means: Correlation is a number from -1 to +1 showing whether two things move together. Regular periods has the strongest link (-0.46, negative because regular periods go with less PCOS), followed by extra hair growth (0.40), dark skin patches (0.36) and weight gain (0.31). Age, height and exercise are close to zero.

    7. Correlation heatmap (everything vs everything)

    What this means: Red squares mean two columns rise together and blue means one rises as the other falls. Weight and BMI are almost the same information (0.89). The symptoms are only mildly linked to each other (about 0.2 to 0.3), so each one adds something new.
    What we learned and how it shaped the model No single sign decides PCOS, so we used a model that combines all the signs. Because PCOS is the smaller group, we gave those cases more weight while training. A link in the data does not prove a cause: for example, our data cannot show that fast food causes PCOS. These results describe our dataset only and are not medical advice.
